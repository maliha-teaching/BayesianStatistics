Conditional posterior of θ given σ² (Section 5.3)

We want the posterior of \(\theta\) when \(\sigma^2\) is treated as known. The plan is to multiply the likelihood by the prior, keep only the factors that involve \(\theta\), and recognize a Normal kernel.

Model. $$Y_i\mid\theta,\sigma^2\overset{iid}{\sim}N(\theta,\sigma^2),\qquad p(\theta,\sigma^2)\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^2}.$$

Proof. Let \(\bar y=\frac1n\sum_i y_i\) and \(s^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2\). The joint posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior:

$$p(\theta,\sigma^2\mid y)\;\propto\;\prod_{i=1}^n(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(y_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}\cdot(\sigma^2)^{-1}.$$

Collect the powers of \(\sigma^2\) and combine the exponentials into one sum:

$$p(\theta,\sigma^2\mid y)\;\propto\;(\sigma^2)^{-(n/2+1)}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}. \tag{1}$$

The sum in (1) mixes \(\theta\) with the data. To separate them, add and subtract \(\bar y\) inside the square and expand:

$$\sum_i(y_i-\theta)^2=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2+2(\bar y-\theta)\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)+n(\bar y-\theta)^2.$$

The middle term is zero because \(\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)=0\). Hence

$$\sum_i(y_i-\theta)^2=(n-1)s^2+n(\theta-\bar y)^2 .$$

Substitute this into (1) and split the exponential into a part without \(\theta\) and a part with \(\theta\):

$$p(\theta,\sigma^2\mid y)\;\propto\;\underbrace{(\sigma^2)^{-(n/2+1)}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(n-1)s^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}}_{\text{does not involve }\theta}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{n(\theta-\bar y)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}. \tag{2}$$

Now fix \(\sigma^2\). By the definition of a conditional density,

$$p(\theta\mid y,\sigma^2)=\frac{p(\theta,\sigma^2\mid y)}{p(\sigma^2\mid y)}.$$

The denominator does not involve \(\theta\), so as a function of \(\theta\) the conditional is proportional to the joint. In (2) the first factor does not involve \(\theta\) either, so it is absorbed into the constant, leaving

$$p(\theta\mid y,\sigma^2)\;\propto\;\exp\Big\{-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}(\theta-\bar y)^2\Big\}=\exp\Big\{-\frac{(\theta-\bar y)^2}{2\,(\sigma^2/n)}\Big\}. \tag{3}$$

A \(N(m,v)\) density is proportional to \(\exp\{-(\theta-m)^2/(2v)\}\). Comparing with (3) gives \(m=\bar y\) and \(v=\sigma^2/n\). A kernel determines the density, because the normalizing constant is forced by \(\int p=1\). Therefore

$$\boxed{\;\theta\mid y,\sigma^2\;\sim\;N\!\Big(\bar y,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big)\;}$$

The posterior is centered at the sample mean and its variance shrinks like \(1/n\). This looks like the sampling distribution of \(\bar Y\), but the roles are reversed: here \(\theta\) is the random quantity and \(\bar y\) is fixed. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

Why an improper prior is allowed

The prior \(1/\sigma^2\) does not integrate to a finite number, so it is not a probability density. Bayes' rule only needs the product of likelihood and prior to be integrable in \((\theta,\sigma^2)\), so that it can be normalized into a proper posterior.

That holds here as long as \(n\ge2\), so that \(s^2>0\). The check is done when we derive the marginal of \(\sigma^2\).

The cross term

Write \(y_i-\theta=(y_i-\bar y)+(\bar y-\theta)\) and square:

$$(y_i-\theta)^2=(y_i-\bar y)^2+2(y_i-\bar y)(\bar y-\theta)+(\bar y-\theta)^2.$$

Sum over \(i\). The factor \((\bar y-\theta)\) does not depend on \(i\), so it comes out of the middle sum, and the last term is added \(n\) times. Also

$$\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)=\sum_i y_i-n\bar y=n\bar y-n\bar y=0 .$$

What counts as a constant

Here "constant" means free of \(\theta\), not free of \(\sigma^2\). We are finding the density of \(\theta\) with \(\sigma^2\) held fixed, so anything that depends only on \(\sigma^2\) and \(y\), such as \((\sigma^2)^{-(n/2+1)}\) and \(\exp\{-(n-1)s^2/(2\sigma^2)\}\), can be dropped.

This would be wrong when finding the marginal of \(\sigma^2\), because there \(\sigma^2\) is the variable.

The normalizing constant

For any \(m\) and \(v>0\),

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(\theta-m)^2/(2v)}\,d\theta=\sqrt{2\pi v}.$$

With \(m=\bar y\) and \(v=\sigma^2/n\), the full conditional density is

$$p(\theta\mid y,\sigma^2)=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi\sigma^2}}\;\exp\Big\{-\frac{n(\theta-\bar y)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}.$$

The factor \(\sqrt{n/(2\pi\sigma^2)}\) depends on \(\sigma^2\). It matters later, when we average this density over the posterior of \(\sigma^2\).
