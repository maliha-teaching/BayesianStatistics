Marginal posterior of θ (Section 5.3)

We want the marginal posterior of \(\theta\). Given \(\tilde\sigma^2=1/\sigma^2\), the parameter \(\theta\) is Normal. But \(\tilde\sigma^2\) is itself uncertain, with a Gamma posterior, so the marginal of \(\theta\) is a Normal averaged over that uncertainty. We show that this average is a Student \(t\) distribution.

Model. $$Y_i\mid\theta,\tilde\sigma^2\overset{iid}{\sim}N\!\Big(\theta,\frac{1}{\tilde\sigma^2}\Big),\quad \theta\mid\tilde\sigma^2\sim N\!\Big(\mu_0,\frac{1}{k_0\tilde\sigma^2}\Big),\quad \tilde\sigma^2\sim\text{Gamma}\!\Big(\frac{\nu_0}{2},\frac{\nu_0\sigma_0^2}{2}\Big)$$

Proof. Let \(k_n=k_0+n\), \(\nu_n=\nu_0+n\), \(\mu_n=\dfrac{k_0\mu_0+n\bar y}{k_n}\), and let \(\sigma_n^2\) be defined by

$$\nu_n\sigma_n^2=\nu_0\sigma_0^2+(n-1)s^2+\frac{nk_0}{k_n}(\bar y-\mu_0)^2 .$$

The two posteriors are

$$\theta\mid\tilde\sigma^2,y\sim N\!\Big(\mu_n,\frac{1}{k_n\tilde\sigma^2}\Big). \tag{A}$$ $$\tilde\sigma^2\mid y\sim\text{Gamma}\!\Big(\frac{\nu_n}{2},\frac{\nu_n\sigma_n^2}{2}\Big)\qquad(\text{shape, rate}). \tag{B}$$

To work with (A), standardize \(\theta\). Define

$$Z=\sqrt{k_n\tilde\sigma^2}\,(\theta-\mu_n).$$

By (A), \(Z\mid\tilde\sigma^2,y\sim N(0,1)\). Solving the definition of \(Z\) for \(\theta\),

$$\theta=\mu_n+\frac{Z}{\sqrt{k_n\tilde\sigma^2}}. \tag{1}$$

Compare \(\theta\) and \(Z\). The distribution of \(\theta\) changes with \(\tilde\sigma^2\): a small \(\tilde\sigma^2\) gives a wide Normal around \(\mu_n\), and a large one gives a narrow Normal. So \(\theta\) is not independent of \(\tilde\sigma^2\). Standardizing removes this dependence. If \(\tilde\sigma^2=0.5\), then \(Z\sim N(0,1)\); if \(\tilde\sigma^2=10\), then \(Z\sim N(0,1)\); the same holds for every other value. The value of \(\tilde\sigma^2\) tells us nothing about \(Z\), so \(Z\) and \(\tilde\sigma^2\) are independent given \(y\), and \(Z\mid y\sim N(0,1)\).

Next we put \(\tilde\sigma^2\) in a recognizable form. Define

$$W=\nu_n\sigma_n^2\,\tilde\sigma^2 .$$

Multiplying a Gamma variable by a constant \(c\) divides its rate by \(c\). Here the rate \(\nu_n\sigma_n^2/2\) in (B) is divided by \(\nu_n\sigma_n^2\), which leaves \(1/2\):

$$W\mid y\sim\text{Gamma}\!\Big(\frac{\nu_n}{2},\frac12\Big)=\chi^2_{\nu_n},\qquad\text{and}\qquad \tilde\sigma^2=\frac{W}{\nu_n\sigma_n^2}. \tag{2}$$

Given \(y\), the number \(\nu_n\sigma_n^2\) is a constant, so \(W\) is a function of \(\tilde\sigma^2\) alone. Since \(Z\) is independent of \(\tilde\sigma^2\), it is independent of \(W\).

Substitute (2) into (1) and pull the constants out of the square root:

$$\theta=\mu_n+\frac{Z}{\sqrt{k_n\,W/(\nu_n\sigma_n^2)}}=\mu_n+\frac{\sigma_n}{\sqrt{k_n}}\cdot\frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/\nu_n}}. \tag{3}$$

In (3), \(Z\sim N(0,1)\), \(W\sim\chi^2_{\nu_n}\), and \(Z\) and \(W\) are independent. By the definition of the \(t\) distribution, \(Z/\sqrt{W/\nu_n}\sim t_{\nu_n}\). Therefore

$$\boxed{\;\frac{\theta-\mu_n}{\sigma_n/\sqrt{k_n}}\;\Big|\;y\;\sim\;t_{\nu_0+n}\;}$$

Equivalently, \(\theta\mid y\) is a location–scale \(t\) with location \(\mu_n\), scale \(\sigma_n/\sqrt{k_n}\), and \(\nu_n=\nu_0+n\) degrees of freedom. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

Gamma scaling

Let \(X\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\) with density \(f_X(x)=\dfrac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}x^{a-1}e^{-bx}\), and let \(Y=cX\) with \(c>0\). Then

$$f_Y(y)=\frac1c f_X\!\Big(\frac yc\Big)=\frac1c\cdot\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\Big(\frac yc\Big)^{a-1}e^{-by/c}=\frac{(b/c)^a}{\Gamma(a)}\,y^{a-1}e^{-(b/c)y}.$$

This is the \(\text{Gamma}(a,b/c)\) density. In our case \(a=\nu_n/2\), \(b=\nu_n\sigma_n^2/2\) and \(c=\nu_n\sigma_n^2\), so \(b/c=1/2\).

Gamma(ν/2, 1/2) is χ²

The \(\text{Gamma}(\nu/2,\,1/2)\) density is

$$\frac{(1/2)^{\nu/2}}{\Gamma(\nu/2)}\,x^{\nu/2-1}e^{-x/2},\qquad x>0,$$

which is the density of the chi-square distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom. Equivalently, \(\chi^2_\nu\) is the distribution of a sum of \(\nu\) squared independent standard Normals.

Only the rate \(1/2\) gives this form. Any other constant \(c\) in the scaling would still give a Gamma, but not a \(\chi^2\). That is why we scale by \(\nu_n\sigma_n^2\).

Definition of t

If \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(W\sim\chi^2_\nu\) are independent, then

$$\frac{Z}{\sqrt{W/\nu}}\sim t_\nu .$$

This is the definition of the Student \(t\) distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, so no further proof is needed. Here \(\nu=\nu_n=\nu_0+n\).

The degrees of freedom are twice the Gamma shape in (B). The extra factor \((\tilde\sigma^2)^{1/2}\) from the prior of \(\theta\) is already used up in turning the joint posterior into the Normal in (A) and the Gamma in (B), so it does not add a degree of freedom.
